ABOUT THIS PROJECT

This is a project about how changing material availability becomes part of seabird behaviour in coastal environments. The Coastal Sites, Material Atlas, and Speculative Scenarios are three ways of tracing that relationship: what materials become available, why birds may incorporate them, and what happens when those materials persist within bird habitats across Scotland's coastal areas.

REPORT 02

Repeated encounters turn debris into familiar material

JULY 2036
FINDHORN / BURGHEAD / TROUP HEAD
MATERIAL FAMILIARITY ACROSS THREE COASTAL SITES

Field observations recorded in Findhorn and Burghead show nests composed almost entirely of plastic-based materials. In both locations, human-made materials now make up a significant part of the surrounding nesting environment, shaped by proximity to settlement, harbour activity, and long-term coexistence between birds and human infrastructure.

By contrast, a comparative nest documented near the residential edge of Troup Head RSPB still contains a noticeably lower proportion of plastic, where natural cliff-based materials remain more available.

REPORT 01

Gannets Are Becoming Plastic Birds

JULY 2036
TROUP HEAD / SCOTLAND
FUTURE 02 — MATERIAL PATHWAYS ARE REDIRECTED

As increasing amounts of plastic continue to enter seabird habitats, northern gannets are being observed interacting with and selecting anthropogenic materials more frequently. A team from the Scottish Seabird Centre has warned that plastic is no longer only present around colonies — it is becoming embedded in nesting behaviour, feeding environments and the material conditions in which young birds develop.

PLASTIC-RELATED EXPOSURE

Researchers have also reported growing concern around long-term plastic exposure and plastic-related disease. One of the most visually striking signs documented in recent breeding seasons is the presence of plastic fragments attached to or embedded around the surfaces of some eggs. The exact mechanism remains under investigation, but researchers are examining whether this may be associated with persistent microplastic exposure within adult birds and their nesting environments.

REPORT 02

Cleaning the Coast Is No Longer Enough

JULY 2036
MORAY COAST / SCOTLAND
01

BEACH CLEANING / REMOVAL

In response to the growing presence of plastic in coastal habitats, Moray Ocean Community is calling on residents to move beyond observation and take part in regular beach-cleaning programmes. Community collection events now remove more than 2,000 pieces of marine litter each year, redirecting waste before it can re-enter beaches, nesting areas and coastal food webs.

2,000+ITEMS / YEARREMOVED FROM COASTAL MATERIAL PATHWAYS
02

REDIRECTION

As seabirds continue to select lightweight, flexible and durable materials, new forms of coastal infrastructure are being proposed to change what becomes available to them. Plans include large communal nesting structures made from recovered plastic, as well as biodegradable one-piece nests produced through PLA 3D printing. These interventions are designed not to force birds away from anthropogenic materials, but to redirect material availability while allowing birds to retain their own nesting choices.
